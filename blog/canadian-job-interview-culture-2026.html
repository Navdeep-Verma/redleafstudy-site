Canadian Job Interview Culture: What Newcomers Get Wrong

The skills aren't the problem — the unwritten rules are. Here's what's actually different, plus a 30-second readiness check.

6 min read · Redleaf Study

Every year, qualified newcomers lose out on roles they were genuinely capable of doing — not because of their skills, but because of small, unspoken cultural mismatches during the interview itself. These aren't things anyone will explicitly tell you; they're just assumed. Here are the ones that trip people up most often.

The Self-Promotion Gap

In many cultures, describing your own achievements directly can feel uncomfortable, even boastful. In a Canadian interview, the opposite is usually true: interviewers expect you to clearly and specifically own your individual contributions — "I led," "I built," "I solved" — rather than deferring credit to a team. Being too modest is frequently misread as a lack of real experience, not humility.

The Salary Question

Being asked "what are your salary expectations?" directly, early in the process, is completely normal in Canada — it isn't a trap, and dodging it repeatedly can actually read as evasive. The expected move is a brief, researched range, delivered plainly, not an uncomfortable deflection. It's also normal and accepted to ask the interviewer for the posted or budgeted range first if it wasn't shared.

A genuinely useful habit: research typical pay for the role and city beforehand (job boards, salary aggregator sites, and your own network are all reasonable sources) so your number is confident and grounded, not guessed on the spot.

Small Talk Isn't Filler — It's Part of the Assessment

The first few minutes of casual conversation before the "real" interview starts often feels, to newcomers, like a delay before the actual evaluation. In Canadian interview culture, it frequently is part of the evaluation — a soft read on communication style and ease. A brief, genuine exchange (weather, commute, something in the office) before moving to business isn't wasted time; skipping straight to business can occasionally read as overly formal or abrupt.

The Follow-Up Norm

A short thank-you email within 24 hours — a genuine line about something specific from the conversation, plus reaffirming interest — is a widely expected, low-cost signal in Canadian hiring. Skipping it isn't disqualifying on its own, but sending one is one of the easiest, highest-leverage things you can do that costs almost nothing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it rude to discuss salary directly? ▾
No. Discussing salary expectations directly, when asked, is standard and expected. A realistic researched range is generally seen as more professional than avoiding the question.
Is it okay to talk about my own achievements? ▾
Yes, and it's expected. Canadian interview culture generally rewards direct, specific self-promotion — describing your own individual contributions clearly is read as confidence, not arrogance, as long as it stays factual.
Should I send a thank-you email afterward? ▾
Yes, a short thank-you email within 24 hours is a widely expected norm and a genuine, low-cost way to stand out.
